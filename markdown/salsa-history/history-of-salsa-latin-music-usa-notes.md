### 1930s: Mario Bauza In Harlem

Status: `included`

Raw notes:

- Mario Bauza was a classically trained clarinetist.
- He fell in love with jazz.
- He came from Cuba, where prejudices held him back.
- In Harlem, he was more free to explore and express himself.
- He felt more free as a Black man in Harlem.
- He played for Chick Webb at the Savoy Ballroom.
- Bauza became the orchestra's musical director.
- Bauza became more familiar with American swing.

Public-page direction:

- Use as the opening bridge card: Bauza learns the language of swing and later applies it to Afro-Cuban rhythm.
- Verify instrument history, Chick Webb/Savoy details, and wording around race/prejudice with reliable sources before public use.

Ledger-only notes:

- The subjective framing that Harlem made him "more free" should be handled carefully and sourced if used publicly.

### Dizzy Gillespie Encounters Afro-Cuban Rhythm

Status: `included`

Raw notes:

- Dizzy Gillespie loved the music.
- He had already revolutionized jazz, but saw its resistance to change.
- He thought the rhythm of jazz might feel "boring."
- In the 1940s, Cuban conga players arrived in NY; one in particular was Candido Camero.
- Candido introduced new levels of conga mastery.
- Dizzy got introduced to Chano Pozo, a conga player with a lot of folkloric knowledge and personality.
- Dizzy asked Chano to stay with Dizzy's band, with a lot of resistance.
- Chano did not speak English.
- Chano made up a song and introduced it to Dizzy: "Manteca."

Public-page direction:

- Use as a dedicated "Dizzy Gillespie + Chano Pozo / 'Manteca'" card.
- Keep the focus on Cubop and Afro-Cuban percussion entering bebop.

Ledger-only notes:

- "Jazz might feel boring" and "with a lot of resistance" need careful sourcing.
- Candido Camero may belong in the ledger or a later musician spotlight rather than the main public card, unless research supports a clean narrative role.

### Brill Building / Latin Influence On Rock And Roll

Status: `included`

Raw notes:

- The Brill Building in NY, where songwriters and producers made music, was near the Palladium.
- It was filled with "mambo knicks" like Doc Thomas, writing hits like "Save the Last Dance for Me," calling their work Jewish Latin.
- Late 1950s into 1960s: influence of Latin music on rock and roll.
- "Louie Louie" was based on a cha-cha-cha written by Rene Touzet.
- "I Can't Get No" also has the cha-cha-cha.
- "Day Tripper" also has the cha-cha-cha.
- "Good Lovin'" by the Young Rascals.

### Willie Colon And Hector Lavoe / El Malo

Status: `included`

Raw notes:

- Willie Colon was a trombonist at the center of the 1970s salsa revolution, starting with Latin boogaloo.
- By 1967, Colon had a record deal with a local label.
- His mom was his representative; she was around 30 years old and had a high-school education.
- Colon hired a singer, Hector Lavoe, a Puerto Rican.
- El Malo was Colon's debut album.
- Puerto Ricans at that time wanted to be "bad."
- Super Fly was out.
- Shaft was out.
- Willie Colon used gangster imagery on records, copying famous movies like The Good, the Bad and the Ugly or The Hustler.
- Colon's music evolved.
- Singer Hector Lavoe had an impact.
- Colon focused more toward Latin influences than boogaloo.
- Hector had a strong Puerto Rican influence.
- Colon and Lavoe had a great band together.
- Colon made a Christmas album with heavy Puerto Rican influence, including the cuatro.
- The album was Asalto Navideno, a big album.

### Ruben Blades / Siembra

Status: `merged`

Raw notes:

- Ruben Blades moved to NY in 1974.
- He stopped being an attorney.
- He called Fania hoping to record and got rejected.
- He became a mailman at the company instead.
- He recorded with Willie Colon as producer.
- Siembra was "the Sgt. Pepper of salsa."
- Siembra sold 500K copies just in Caracas, Venezuela.
- Problems in Panama, political problem, and the album seemed to come at a great time.
- One famous song on the record: "Mack the Knife."
- Siembra was the beginning of the end of Fania Records' domination.
